A Nondual Approach
to Goals

When action is organized from openness.

The New Year arrives with familiar energy — resolutions, plans, the collective insistence that this time will be different. Attention moves to a future self who is fitter, more creative, and more at peace.

A student of nonduality doesn't begin by arguing for or against goals. The inquiry is simply: from where does the impulse for change arise?

One orientation is organized around the sense of a separate "me" — the feeling that something must be added or secured in order to be safe, worthy, or whole. Goals become a strategy for securing what feels missing. This is separation bias operating through ambition.

The other is organized from openness — the intelligence of life moving without needing an outcome to prove anything. This movement can include planning, structure, and strategy. But it holds everything lightly, because it is not trying to prove itself through the outcome.

The same resolution or goal — writing a post, starting a business, changing a habit — can arise from either orientation. From the outside, the actions may look identical. From the inside, they do not.

What the Difference Feels Like

When action is organized around the "me," it often carries weight, urgency, contraction, a sense that something essential is on the line. The future implores, and the present becomes a means to an end. Beneath the effort is a subtle pressure to "become" enough.

When action is organized from openness, there can be ease even in effort, and stability even in the midst of fear. The difficulty may remain, but the internal war against it ends. Work happens because contribution is natural when life is not at war with itself.

What Stalling Tells Us

If a goal has appeared on a list for years and still feels out of reach, the stalling itself is information. Whatever has felt survivable has already been done. What remains is often what the separate self experiences as identity-threatening: the change that might bring exposure, loss of control, judgment, or the end of a familiar story about who one is.

That isn't failure. It is the system doing what it was trained to do.

So the practice is to notice any sense of shame in the delay. Notice the ultimate source — and include what arises without giving it the final say.

Is this movement being asked from lack? Or is it arising as a clear responsiveness to what is beneficial right now?

Goals as Provisional Directions

When that distinction is seen, the New Year becomes less a campaign to improve the self and more a practice of attunement. The goal may remain. The direction may stay clear. But attachment to form loosens. Structure can stay without becoming coercive.

From here, goals become provisional directions — ways of organizing time and attention — without becoming demands placed on life. A stalled goal isn't automatically a failure of will. Conditions may not be right. Fear may remain while trust deepens anyway.

And when action comes, it comes more subtly. Less dramatic. Less self-violent. It doesn't need to announce itself as change. It simply happens as the next step becomes visible and workable.

The goal and the ground are not two. There is only the next indicated step, and the willingness to take it.
