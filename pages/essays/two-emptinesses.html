Two Emptinesses

Judgment and its root. On separation bias and ontological misreading.

After years of practice, study, and watching how suffering is built in real time, I've found it useful to distinguish two "emptinesses" as they are emphasized, experienced, and taught.

Empty of judgment — empty of the overlay that calls something good or bad, right or wrong, worthy or unworthy.

Empty of an independent nature — empty of "standalone" existence; nothing existing as a separate, self-established unit.

The first emptiness points to an evaluative overlay; the second is about the root perceptual error that makes everything seem separate enough to judge.

Emptiness as the Absence of Judgment

The first meaning is practical and immediate. An experience arises, then thought adds an interpretation and a verdict, most likely based on past conditioning. The verdict may be subtle ("this is off"), loud ("this is horrible"), or self-referential ("it's all my fault"). But the structure is consistent: something appears, and thought concludes what it means to a "separate" me.

To be "empty of judgment" doesn't mean becoming indifferent. It means the evaluative overlay is thin or not granted authority. The nervous system can still activate, decisions can still be made, but the moment is no longer organized around prosecution and defense.

Emptiness as the Absence of Standalone Existence

The second meaning is ontological. In Buddhist philosophy, "emptiness" (śūnyatā) points to the absence of self-established essence. This teaching does not say that nothing exists. It says that nothing exists separately.

A thing exists dependently: through causes, conditions, parts, naming, contrast, context, perception, function. When you look for "chair" as a standalone essence — chairness existing in itself — you find parts, materials, sensations, purposes, and the concept "chair" doing its work.

Judgment Depends on Separation Bias

Judgment is not merely a mental habit. It rests on separation bias — the belief that experience is actually made of separate parts. To judge something as good or bad, thought must first successfully isolate it. It has to take a fluid, interdependent process and freeze it into a standalone unit. Only once thought has created this separate entity can it attach a verdict to it.

Separation bias is how the ontological error operates. When this is seen, judgment loses its target. The verdict isn't avoided or suppressed; it just loses its authority. It is recognized as commentary on a map drawn by a biased instrument, not a statement about the territory itself.

But Ethics and Discernment Remain

Emptiness doesn't erase discernment. It removes false solidity. On the conventional level, actions have effects. Kindness tends to soothe. Cruelty tends to harm. A child needs care. All of this remains true.

And when interdependence is actually seen, ethics can become less performative and more obvious. Harm is not ultimately "to an other" who exists separately; it is harm to the field — harm to oneself as well. Compassion becomes less conditional and more direct.

In the end, emptiness isn't an attainment. It's the removal of a misreading: the assumption that life is made of separate nouns, each carrying a verdict, each threatening or promising a separate "me."

When that assumption relaxes, something simpler becomes possible: life can be met without turning every appearance into a conclusion of winning and losing a game that was never required.
