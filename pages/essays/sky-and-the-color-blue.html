Sky and the Color Blue

On separation bias and the restlessness beneath success.

There's a particular kind of restlessness I notice in many of the senior leaders I work with — people who have accomplished much, refined themselves for big things, and by all accounts "done the work." It's not the raw dissatisfaction of a life in crisis. It's more subtle. The feeling that something essential remains just beyond reach, that happiness has somehow eluded them and they can't quite understand why.

This unease isn't born of neglect. It persists after years of coaching, therapy, remarkable success. These are people who lead teams, build companies, raise families. Many dedicate hours to meditation. Their lives are full and privileged by most measures. Yet that faint sense of something missing lingers — the feeling that peace lives somewhere else, always just ahead.

I've come to see that this restlessness points to something deeper than unmet needs or unfinished inner work. It reveals an assumption we rarely question, something I've started calling "separation bias."

This is the assumption that we are apart — from our true nature, from the natural world, from each other, from any sense of wholeness. It's the feeling that you are a separate "me" looking at a separate "everything else." This bias runs so deep it feels like reality itself. Spiritual traditions have recognized this for millennia as illusion or maya, but calling it a "bias" feels less judgmental and more workable, especially when I'm sitting with a successful CEO who wonders why fulfillment feels so elusive.

Like all biases, it hides in plain sight — a lens we look through, never at.

What We Were Born With

There's another way of seeing — one that children know naturally before they learn concepts of separation. My teacher calls it "basic space" — the pure awareness that is the source of all experience.

Everything that appears in this space — every thought, feeling, sensation, person, place, thing — isn't separate from the space itself.

The color blue isn't a separate object inside the sky. It is the sky appearing as blue. In the same way, awareness doesn't contain thoughts and feelings as separate objects. It appears as thoughts and feelings — expressions of the awareness that knows them.

When we identify as a separate person viewing separate objects, we create two imaginary reference points. When we rest as the essence of this so-called basic space, seeing simply becomes seeing. We recognize we were never contained by any reference point at all.

The Opening

For me, this recognition began with noticing the restlessness beneath my own experience. I was in my twenties with a prestigious education, good work, close relationships. Yet the question persisted: "Why, with all this, am I not deeply happy?"

It wasn't a demand for an answer. More like a wondering that opened space. That question became the first glimpse of awakening in what I now see was a trance — the belief that fulfillment was always just ahead, one more achievement or insight away.

Recognition arrived when I saw what was driving everything: not that I was disconnected from joy, but that I believed I was disconnected from the very source of joy — my own nature as basic space, as awareness, as the intelligence of life appearing as all of this.

What Becomes Simple

The beautiful thing I found is that seeking and not-seeking aren't separate. The space and what appears in space aren't separate. Awareness and its contents arise together as one seamless movement.

When this is seen, something simple returns. There's no separate person who needs to get anywhere, and nothing to be done to arrive at the peace that was never actually missing.

We don't avoid difficulty or collapse into it. We simply listen and respond. Gratitude appears naturally. Our capacity to contribute expands. Life doesn't become bigger or smaller — just simpler.

There is only one presence here, appearing now as all of this.

Like seeing that the sky and the color blue are never two.
